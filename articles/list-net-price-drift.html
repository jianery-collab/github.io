Pricing Strategy

Two lines that drifted apart

Discounts vary across a product line. Should you fix the list price?

Jan Y. Yang · pricinggoat.com

In a well-run product line, list prices and net prices move in proportion. The products share the same discount logic, so the ratio between what you ask and what you get stays the same from the cheapest item to the most expensive one. Plot both against product value and the two lines tell a coherent story.

Then time passes, and a few years later the story no longer matches. Some products sell at 15% off while their siblings go at 35%. Nobody decided this. It just happened one deal at a time, for reasons that each made sense at the moment. The customer mix shifted. Buying behavior changed. A product found new use cases. A cost shock hit one component. Some products started going out in bundles with items from other lines, which came with their own discounting habits.

It looks out of order and the temptation is obvious: adjust the list prices and bring back the old harmony. In most cases, that's the wrong move.

Which line moved?

Before touching anything, take a step back to recall what the two lines represent. The list price is your statement of value. The net price is what the market actually pays. When they drift apart, one of two things has happened. Either value has changed, or discounting has drifted away from value. These two call for opposite responses, and confusing them is costly.

If you rewrite the list price every time discounting drifts, your value statement ends up following your sales team's habits.

Most drift isn't about value

Customer mix. Different customers buy different products and get different discounts. Add them all up and the overall picture bends, even if each segment is perfectly consistent on its own. The first step is to look segment by segment. Quite often, the problem turns out to be smaller than it looked, or not there at all.

Bundling works in a similar fashion. When a bundle discount gets booked against one product, that product looks heavily discounted, while the real cause sits elsewhere in the bundle. That's an allocation problem, not a pricing problem. Fix how bundle discounts are spread across line items, and the drift often shrinks a great deal.

Leakage. Some products are simply easier to give away, and sales discounts them more because nobody pushes back. Here, adjusting the list price makes things worse either way. Cut it and you make the leakage official. Raise it to protect the net price and the discount gets even bigger. In both cases you reward the behavior you should be correcting. This belongs to discount governance: clearer rules, proper approvals, and deal reviews that actually happen.

Cost shocks deserve their own decision. Cost is not value. A lasting cost increase may well justify a price move, but that's a question of margin and positioning, to be decided on its own terms rather than as a way to tidy up a chart.

When the list price really is wrong

There is one case where the list price is the problem. If customers now use a product differently and get more or less value from it, the product has moved on the value scale. The old list price no longer reflects what it's worth, and it should move. This is the only cause in the list that changes value itself, and it's also the rarest.

Why list prices deserve extra caution

A list price is not just a number on a price sheet. Customers treat it as a reference point. Channels build their margins on it. Competitors benchmark against it. Contracts, rebates and escalation clauses are often written around it. Change a list price and everything tied to it moves with it. Correct a discount instead and you can target exactly the deals, customers or products that drifted.

So the order matters:

Fix the drift in this order
1.
Confirm the drift is real by looking at it segment by segment.
2.
Clean up how bundle discounts are allocated.
3.
Tighten governance wherever net prices drifted without a value reason.
4.
Only then re-anchor list prices, and only where value has truly changed.

After that, keep an eye on the ratio between net and list across the product line, so the next drift is caught in months rather than years.

The bottom line

The ratio between list and net is a signal, not a decoration. When it breaks, it's telling you something about your customers, your deals or your products. Listen to what it says before you redraw the list.

Harmony should come from discipline in net prices, not from list prices bent to fit the mess.

Pricing by design, not by default.
